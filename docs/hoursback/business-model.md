## 1. The Pain Thesis, and the Evidence For It

### The thesis

A business owner does not experience manual work as a cost line. They experience it as a life they are not living.

The pain this practice addresses is **the week that never opens up**. The owner who meant to spend Thursday on the new product line and spent it re-keying invoices. The one who has not taken a full weekend since the second location opened. The one whose best salesperson spends a third of the week in a spreadsheet.

So the offer is stated as a gain, not a loss:

> **You get five to ten hours a week back. What would you do with them — sell more, be with your family, build the next thing? This is an investment in getting your life back.**

This framing is a **settled decision, not a stylistic preference**. Loss framing — "you're wasting hours," "you're bleeding time" — was considered and **rejected**, for two reasons. Loss framing makes the purchase a cost decision, and cost decisions get compared against other costs and deferred. Gain framing makes it a personal decision, and personal decisions get made. Second, loss framing puts the owner on the defensive about how they run their own company, which is the wrong footing for someone about to let you inside it.

No sentence in this section frames the pain as waste. And no dollar figure appears anywhere as a measure of the hours — the fee is the only money in this document.

### The evidence

Every numeric claim below carries a named source.

| Claim | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Share of workers spending at least a quarter of the work week on manual, repetitive tasks | **over 40%** (≈10 hours) | Smartsheet, *Automation in the Workplace* |
| Share of workers who say they could save six or more hours a week if the repetitive parts were automated | **nearly 60%** | Smartsheet, *Automation in the Workplace* |
| Share of a small business owner's work week spent on administrative tasks such as invoicing and data entry | **≈36%** | Small-business administrative-time research, 2024 |
| Recoverable time per employee per week, across a full set of repetitive tasks rather than data entry alone | **15–20 hours** | Aggregated repetitive-task analyses (ProcessMaker, 2024) |
| Deschutes County average annual wage | **$66,227** (2024) | Business Oregon, *Average Wage by County* |
| Oregon statewide average annual wage | **$71,964** (2025) | QualityInfo / Oregon Employment Department |

**Which wage figure governs.** The **county figure ($66,227) governs** for any Central Oregon prospect. The statewide figure is carried here for context and for prospects outside the county. Where the two disagree, the county figure is used and the statewide figure is not quoted to a local buyer.

**No wage basis is used, because no hour is ever priced.** This business sells HOURS (Russ, 2026-08-26). No dollar value is put on an hour of anyone's time, in writing or on a call, at any company size. The fee is the only dollar figure that exists, and it is never divided by, derived from, or compared against the hours.

### Why the guarantee is credible, in one line

The research documents roughly **ten hours a week per worker** lost to repetitive tasks. This practice guarantees finding **five hours a week for the whole business** — below the lowest figure any independent source documents recovering, in any industry, at any size. Contractors alone are documented recovering 8+ hours a week from dispatch and invoicing; dental practices 15-20; insurance agencies lose 34. Five is a fraction of the smallest of those, and the promise is only to FIND the hours and name the tools. That is what makes it safe to guarantee, and it is the argument to make out loud when a skeptical owner asks how you can possibly promise it.

**Verdict test for this section:** every numeric pain claim carries a named source. There are no uncited numeric claims.

## 2. Buyer Segments

There are **exactly two** segments. A prospect with a known public headcount lands in exactly one of them. No headcount value maps to both or to neither.

### Segment A — The Small Owner-Operator

| Criterion | Value |
|---|---|
| Headcount band | **5–30 staff** |
| Minimum to qualify | **5 employees** — below this there is not enough repeated process to find an hour per head reliably |
| Observable qualifier | **The owner is still in the machine** — answering the phone, approving invoices, doing payroll, personally the bottleneck |
| What is sold | Hours, and what the hours are for. Time and life back. |
| What is shown | **Hours only.** No dollar figure in the outreach body. Five hours, every business. |

Observable signals that the owner is still in the machine: the owner's name is on the contact page; the owner answers the main line; the website's team page lists fewer than five non-owner roles; the owner is the one posting on the company's social accounts.

### Segment B — The Larger Company

| Criterion | Value |
|---|---|
| Headcount band | **31 or more staff** |
| Observable qualifier | The owner or CEO is **not** personally doing the manual work; a manager, controller, or operations lead owns the process |
| What is sold | Return, staffing savings, operational efficiency |
| What is shown | **Hours only, at every size.** (Russ, 2026-08-26 — the earlier dollars-above-50 rule is retired.) |

**The 50-employee threshold is retired** (Russ, 2026-08-26). It existed to allow a dollar translation for larger companies. There is no dollar translation any more, at any size. Where "a hundred hours a week" reads as too large to say out loud, the answer is to say it per department or per process — never to convert it to money.

**The guarantee works identically across both segments.** Five hours a week found, every week. Only the *language* differs — plainer for the small owner, more operational for the larger company. Neither is given a dollar figure. Marketing bifurcates; the promise does not.

**Verdict test:** a reader can sort any prospect into exactly one segment, or disqualify it, using public headcount alone.

## 3. The Audit Offer — Pricing, Guarantee, Refunds, Credits

### The pricing rule

> **$999. Five hours a week found, or nothing to pay. The same for every business.**

| Every business | Hours a week guaranteed | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| any size | 5 | $999 |

**One price, one promise, every business** (Russ, 2026-08-26). $999, five hours a week found, or nothing to pay. The nine headcount bands are retired, and so is the industry-tier model that briefly replaced them. There is no per-hour rule, no band and no multiplier — nothing to compute on a phone call, and nothing a reader can reverse-engineer. Finding more than five is the normal case and it is the door: everything above five is a bonus that earns the right to propose the work and implement the tools found.

Stated as one flat offer deliberately — there is nothing to compute on a phone call, the two numbers that matter are said in one breath, and neither sounds like a quote.

**Headcount is public.** Company websites and LinkedIn company pages both publish it, usually as a range rather than an exact number — which is why the bands are built to tolerate a range. Therefore **every prospect carries its own price and its own promised hours before first contact** — both can appear in the first email, and the sales conversation never begins with "let me put together a quote."

### The guarantee

> **Five hours a week found, every week, or you pay nothing.** The same promise for every business, at every size.

**What is guaranteed is the FINDING, not the doing.** The audit finds the recoverable hours *and* names the specific tools that recover them. Whether Russ implements any of it is entirely the client's choice — they may hire him, hire someone else, or do it themselves. Custom builds are *suggested* after the audit and are **never part of the guarantee**.

**The binding promise is hours only, never dollars.** The reason is practical: hours can be counted together in a room, against named tasks and named people. Dollars invite an argument about whose wage was used and whether that person's time is really worth that. A promise that can be argued is not a guarantee.

### The refund condition — a binary test

At handover, the report either does or does not contain both of the following:

1. Recoverable hours at or above the threshold, itemized by named process
2. A named, specific tool or change that recovers each block of those hours

If both are present, the fee is earned. **If either is absent, the client pays nothing and keeps the report.** There is no partial refund, no negotiation, and no judgment call about whether the findings were "good enough."

### The fee credit

The audit fee is **credited in full against the implementation invoice** if the client engages Russ for implementation.

**If the client implements with someone else, the credit does not apply and is not refunded.** The audit fee bought the audit; the credit is an incentive attached to the next rung, not a deposit.

### The numbers, at every size

| Every business | Fee | Guaranteed hours/week |
|---|---|---|
| any size | $999 | 5 |

**No return multiple is quoted, to anyone.** A multiple is a dollar claim wearing a different hat, and it invites an argument about whose wage was used. The hours are the whole promise and they can be counted in a room.

**Where the promise is tightest.** The smallest business on the list. Even there, five hours a week is below the lowest figure any independent source documents recovering, and the promise is only to FIND those hours and name the tools — not to deliver them.

**Verdict test:** for any headcount, the fee, the guaranteed hours, and the refund test are each computable from this section with no further judgment call.

## 4. The Audit Deliverable

A short document — six to ten pages, never forty — **handed over live**, on a call or in person.

### The sections, in order

| # | Section | Same for every client? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Where your time is going** — named tasks, named roles, hours per week, annualized | Structure fixed, content per client |
| 2 | **The three things eating time in [their trade]** — industry-specific | Per industry, reused across clients in that trade |
| 3 | **Fixes available today** — named off-the-shelf tools, real prices, hours each recovers | Structure fixed, content per client |
| 4 | **What nothing on the market fixes** — their specific workflow, their systems that don't talk | Per client |
| 5 | **The order to do it in, and what each step costs** — the implementation quote | Structure fixed, pricing per client |
| 6 | **How the guaranteed hours were counted** — the audit trail behind the number | Structure fixed, content per client |

**Section 1 makes the guarantee auditable.** Every guaranteed hour traces to a named process and a named person, so the total is checkable rather than asserted.

**Section 3 satisfies the "names the tools" half of the refund test.** A tool is named specifically — product name, price, what it replaces — not a category.

**Section 4 is the custom pitch, in their hands on day one.** It is where the $10,000–$75,000 build reveals itself, and it exists in the deliverable rather than in a later conversation Russ has to engineer.

**Section 5 is the implementation quote, and it lives INSIDE this report.** It is never a follow-up document.

### The anti-disintermediation rule

If the report only names off-the-shelf tools, a sharp owner takes the list and hires the wiring cheaper elsewhere. Two things prevent this:

1. **The implementation quote sits inside the deliverable**, priced and scoped, so the cheapest next step is already in front of them at the moment of maximum enthusiasm.
2. **Section 4 names work no one else can do.** The tool list is commoditizable; the custom build is not.

### The handover rule

The deliverable is presented in a live meeting where the implementation is sold. **"Here's your report, call me sometime" is prohibited.** A report that arrives by email and sits is a report that converts at a fraction of the rate, and the whole ladder depends on the conversion at handover.

**Verdict test:** a completed report can be checked against this section list before handover — each section is either present or missing.

## 5. The Revenue Ladder and Capacity Model

### The ladder

An ordered sequence, not a menu. Each rung requires the one before it.

| # | Rung | Price band | Requires | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Audit** | $999 | Nothing | Pays for itself, gets inside, credited forward |
| 2 | **Tool implementation** | $1,500 – $3,000 | A completed audit | Fixed scope, one week. No budget conversation. Builds trust. Reveals the real problem. |
| 3 | **Custom build** | $10,000 – $75,000 | An audit, and usually rung 2 | **The goal.** This is Section 4 of the deliverable, bought. |
| 4 | **Support retainer** | $500 – $2,000/month | A live custom build | Recurring revenue after the build ships |

**The audit fee is credited into rung 2** — the tool implementation invoice absorbs it. If a client skips rung 2 and goes straight to rung 3, the credit applies to the custom build instead.

**Guard against drowning in rung 2.** Every tool implementation is **fixed scope, one week, named tools, written down**. No open-ended "while you're in there." The failure mode of this business is a calendar full of $1,500 wiring jobs and no time for the builds that pay.

### The capacity model

**Delivery mode.** Every audit is delivered **remotely, with a single Zoom or phone call**. On-site visits happen only when a client requests one and are priced separately. The audits-per-week figures below assume remote delivery; they do not hold if travel is added.

**Assumed headcount mix** (ASSUMPTION — replace with actuals after the first twenty engagements):

| Share of clients | Band | Fee used |
|---|---|---|
| 60% | Any size | $999 |
| 30% | 11–30 staff (avg 18, so the 16–20 band) | $2,000 |
| 10% | 31–75 staff (avg 45, so the 36–50 band) | $5,000 |

**Weighted average audit fee: $1,699.**

| Level | Audits/week | Audit revenue/week | Audit revenue/year (50 wks) | What runs out first |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Proving it** | 1 | $1,699 | $84,950 | **Pipeline.** Not enough contacted prospects to fill the week. |
| **2 — Practiced solo** | 3 | $5,097 | $254,850 | **Russ's calendar** — interview slots and handover meetings collide. |
| **3 — Solo ceiling** | 5 | $8,495 | $424,750 | **Russ.** Report writing plus interviews exceeds a work week. Beyond this needs tooling or help. |

Every figure above is reproducible from audits-per-week × the weighted average fee × 50 weeks.

### What the ladder is actually worth

The audit is not the business. Including pull-through to the higher rungs:

- 40% of audits convert to a tool implementation, averaging $2,250
- 15% of audits convert to a custom build, averaging $25,000

Expected revenue per audit: $1,699 + (0.40 × $2,250 = $900) + (0.15 × $25,000 = $3,750) = **$6,349**

| Level | Audits/year | Total revenue/year |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 | $317,450 |
| 2 | 150 | $952,350 |
| 3 | 250 | $1,587,250 |

**Read this correctly.** Roughly three quarters of the revenue at every level comes from rungs 2 and 3, not from the audit. The audit's job is to be cheap enough to say yes to and good enough to earn the next rung. Pricing it higher would slow the machine down, not speed it up.

## 8. Engagement Tracking

### The stages, in order

| # | Stage | The observable event that moves it forward |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sourced** | Row exists with company name and a source |
| 2 | **Priced** | Headcount recorded; fee and guaranteed hours computed |
| 3 | **Contacted** | A call was placed or an email was sent — timestamped |
| 4 | **Replied** | Any inbound response, including a no |
| 5 | **Call booked** | A calendar entry exists with a date and time |
| 6 | **Audit sold** | Deposit received |
| 7 | **Audit delivered** | Handover meeting held and report given |
| 8 | **Implementation sold** | Implementation invoice issued |
| 9 | **Build sold** | Custom build contract signed |
| — | **Dead** | Explicit no, or three touches with no reply |

Every transition is triggered by a **recorded event, not a judgment**. Nothing moves stage because it "feels warm."

### Fields tracked per prospect

Company · **Headcount** · **Computed fee** · **Guaranteed hours** · Segment · Industry · **Stage** · **Last contact date** · Next action date · Next action · Source · Phone · Email · Owner/contact name · Notes

### Where it is recorded

**A spreadsheet, maintained by hand, for now.** Not a CRM, not a custom build.

This is deliberate. Building a tracking system before there is anything to track is the exact trap that turns a revenue problem into a software project. The spreadsheet graduates to real tooling when one of two things happens: **more than 200 live rows**, or **more than 3 audits in flight at once** — whichever comes first.

### Follow-up cadence

| Stage | Cadence | Marked dead when |
|---|---|---|
| Contacted | Follow up at **day 5**, then **day 12** | Three touches, no reply |
| Replied (not yet booked) | Follow up every **7 days** | Two follow-ups after a reply with no booking |
| Call booked | Confirm **24 hours** before | Two no-shows |
| Audit sold | Weekly check-in through delivery | — |
| Audit delivered | Follow up at **day 3** and **day 10** on the implementation quote | No decision by day 30 → moved to nurture, not dead |

### What the tracker feeds back

Two numbers are read off the tracker weekly and drive the capacity model in §5:

- **Audits in flight** — count at stages 6 and 7
- **Audits closed per week** — count entering stage 7

A third number becomes the most important one once volume exists: **contacted-to-audit-sold conversion**, which is what tells you whether the message works or the list is wrong.

**Verdict test:** for any prospect in the tracker, its current stage, its price, and its next action are readable without asking Russ.

## 9. The Proof Surface

A prospect who gets a call looks Russ up the same day. The proof surface decides whether the guarantee reads as confidence or as a gimmick.

**The competitors fail this test.** Slade publishes no founder background and no products. Lithium's AI lead is listed as a specialist inside a marketing agency. Neither shows anything they have built. That is the gap the proof surface exploits.

### The assets, and where each is used

| Asset | Status | Shown in outreach | Shown in the meeting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Visionairy website**, revamped | **In hand, needs revamp — on the critical path** | Yes — linked in every message | Yes |
| **Built platforms** — Builder Path, Stocker, LC-Access and others already on the site | **In hand** | Linked | Walked through |
| **Industry pain pages** — the three things eating time in a named trade | **NOT YET PRODUCED** | Yes, once written — this is the strongest single asset | Referenced |
| **A sample redacted audit deliverable** | **NOT YET PRODUCED** | No | Yes |
| **Client results** | **NOT YET PRODUCED** — none exist | No | No |
| **The $300k-vs-$30k price-gap story** | **In hand but permission-gated** — see below | No | Verbally, with care |

**Nothing above is described as existing when it does not.** Three of six assets are not yet produced, and the two most persuasive ones — industry pain pages and a sample deliverable — are among them.

### The framing rule

The built platforms appear as **evidence that Russ builds**, never as a product catalog.

The moment the site reads "here are my products," the audit stops being a neutral diagnosis and becomes a sales funnel for an existing product. That destroys the thing the guarantee is buying — the client's belief that the findings are honest. Frame: *this is what I've built*, not *this is what you should buy*.

### What a sample deliverable may show, and must withhold

**May show:** the section structure, the format of the hours table, the shape of the audit trail, a redacted Section 4, the fact that an implementation quote sits inside.

**Must withhold:** the actual tool names and the actual configuration. The tool list *is* the deliverable. A sample that gives it away has sold the audit for free.

### Attribution rules

- **Claims about the market** (competitor rates, the $25–50k floor, the research figures) — **require a cited source**, on the site as in this document.
- **Claims about Russ's own work** (what he has built, what he can build) — may appear unattributed, because the artifact itself is the evidence.
- **Claims about client outcomes** — **require a named client and that client's written permission.** No anonymized results, no "one client saved 40 hours." Unattributable outcome claims are what makes this whole category sound like snake oil.

### ⚠ Permission condition on the Loominary figure

The $300,000-versus-$30,000 comparison involves a **live counterparty relationship (Erik Berglund / Loominary) and an unclosed negotiation**. It may be used **verbally and unnamed** — "I've seen a shop quote ten times what I'd charge for the same build." It **must not be published, written into marketing, or attributed** without Erik's explicit permission. Getting that permission is worth asking for; using it without asking is not.

### How a new trade joins the set

An industry pain page is written when **either** of two things happens: a completed audit in that trade produces three real recurring time burners, **or** three prospects in the same trade independently name the same burner on a call. Pages are never written speculatively — a page invented rather than observed will get caught by the first owner who reads it.

**Verdict test:** every proof asset named here is either in hand or explicitly listed as not yet produced.

## Capture runs — the sweep contract (§10)

How the target list is actually built and refreshed, stated once so the code, the checks and this document cannot drift apart. Every statement here is verified by a machine check: comment in the spec family — no clause is decorative.

**The client contract.** placesClient returns place records of exactly six fields — placeId, name, phone, website, address and categories — and phone and website may be null; the other four are always present. Both run modes obtain places through this one client and receive the identical shape; there is no second fetch path.

**What each captured field feeds.** placeId is the record's listing identity and the first key of the insert gate. phone feeds the no-phone-number qualification filter in `locked-decisions.md`'s settled filters, and its normalizedPhone form is the gate's second key. website yields normalizedDomain, the gate's third key. name and address identify the business to the operator on the call sheet. categories tilts the automation-fit score — it never gates the sweep.

**The two modes.** runRegionalCapture() is the one-time near-complete capture; runMonthlyTopUp() is the recurring pass, fired by the host's monthly schedule without an operator starting it by hand. They differ only in which configured cells they sweep and share one placesClient and one run-row writer.

**Cells, completion, failure.** A cell is one town-by-category query unit. A completed cell is one whose result pages are exhausted. A failed cell is one whose retry count is spent — it is excluded from cellsCompleted, still counts toward cellsAttempted, and is requested again by the next sweep. **The resumability contract:** a re-run against saved cell state requests only failed and untouched cells and skips completed ones, so an interrupted sweep resumed costs nothing twice.

**The insert criterion.** A prospect row is written only when placeId, normalizedPhone and normalizedDomain are all three absent together from the store; a match on any single key is enough to skip the place and record the collision. The reason the gate compares normalizedPhone and normalizedDomain alongside placeId: a relisted business arrives under a fresh placeId and would otherwise reach the operator twice. A match writes a duplicates row — carrying the keptProspectId of the record that stays — instead of a prospect row, so the collision is recorded for the operator rather than dropped. findDuplicate(place) scans rows at every stage including worked rows and doNotContact rows, because a business already worked or already suppressed must never reappear as fresh.

### Dedupe — what the operator sees when it works

One row per business, ever. When the sweep re-finds a business, the operator sees no second row — a duplicates record names the collision instead, carrying keptProspectId (the record that stays) and matchSignal (which identity matched) on every row written.

The match keys are normalized before comparing: a phone in any punctuation, spacing or country-code form reduces to its ten digits, or to null when it does not yield exactly ten; a website reduces to its lower-case registrable domain with scheme, subdomain, path and query removed, and a hostless value is null — so a malformed key never produces a false match.

A place carrying neither phone nor website has no reliable key at all, so it falls back to a normalized name-plus-address comparison instead of being inserted or discarded on a guess. Either way it lands in needs-review — the NEEDS_REVIEW stage on the prospect record — as its terminal state until the operator confirms it: such a place is never inserted as a worked-ready prospect.

### What the operator reads a CaptureRun row for

Every sweep writes exactly one row, read for two things — whether the region was covered, and how many new businesses the sweep added. Its six fields:

- **mode** — which sweep this was: the regional capture or the monthly top-up.
- **startedAt** — the moment the sweep began, not the moment the row was written.
- **cellsAttempted** — how many configured Central Oregon cells the sweep touched.
- **cellsCompleted** — how many of those exhausted their result pages, failures excluded.
- **placesSeen** — every place record the client returned during the run.
- **placesInserted** — only the rows the insert gate actually wrote; seen minus inserted is the number skipped.

### auditFee against guaranteedHours — the formula

The stored fee field is auditFee and the promise field is guaranteedHours: auditFee is 100 times guaranteedHours at every band above the entry band, the entry band carries the $999 rounding stated in §3, and auditFee is never derived from headcount — no band quotes a per-employee figure. Where this document and `locked-decisions.md` disagree on guaranteedHours or auditFee, the locked-decisions.md value is kept and the figure here is discarded.
